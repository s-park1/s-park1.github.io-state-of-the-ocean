Mission concept 03 · Deep-sea mining and bottom trawling

Measure the deep before we disturb it.

Decisions about mining the deep seafloor for cobalt and other metals are being made now, and bottom trawling already scrapes deep habitat every day. This mission builds the independent baseline both debates are missing: what lives there, how it recovers, and how far the damage spreads.

Region: Clarion-Clipperton Zone, Pacific, plus global trawl mapping Planned length: ~24 days at sea Author: Sungwon Park
This is a planning exercise, and it doesn't take a side. Whether to mine the deep sea is a contested question with serious arguments on both sides. This mission doesn't answer it. It plans how to collect the evidence that any responsible answer needs, and makes that evidence open to everyone.
Where things stand

A decision with a short clock and a thin baseline

The Clarion-Clipperton Zone, a vast abyssal plain in the Pacific between Hawaii and Mexico, is covered in potato-sized nodules rich in cobalt, nickel, copper, and manganese. As of July 2026, the International Seabed Authority still had not adopted rules for mining them, and 46 countries had called for a moratorium or precautionary pause [1]. Meanwhile, NOAA found a U.S. company's application to mine about 65,000 km² of the zone complete in May 2026 and moved it toward an environmental impact statement [2].

The case for mining Supporters argue that nodules could supply metals for batteries and electrification while reducing reliance on land mines concentrated in a few countries, which carry their own environmental and human costs [3].
The case for waiting Opponents argue that the deep sea is poorly studied, recovers on timescales of decades or longer, and could suffer irreversible harm before anyone understands what is being lost [1], [4].
What we know about recovery

The deep heals slowly, when it heals at all

Two kinds of disturbance scrape the deep seafloor: mining collectors that strip nodules from abyssal plains, and bottom trawls that drag across seamounts and slopes. The best long-term evidence for both points the same way.

DisturbanceWhat was found
Nodule test mining, 1979Revisited in 2023, the track was still an 8 m-wide strip cleared of nodules. Small mobile animals had begun to return, but large animals that attach to the seafloor remained rare [4].
Seamount trawling, New ZealandA seamount closed to trawling in 2001 showed no sign of recovery after 14 years; its community still resembled an actively trawled one. Deep corals grow about 1 mm a year [5].
Trawling and carbonOne study estimates 55–60% of the carbon trawls stir from sediments reaches the atmosphere within about nine years [6]. Other fisheries scientists dispute the assumptions behind that estimate [6], which is exactly the kind of gap better measurement can close.

Cobalt also forms in crusts on seamount flanks, mostly between 800 and 2,500 m in the Pacific, growing over millions of years [7]. Seamounts are also where deep-sea trawling concentrates, so the two pressures can land on the same habitat.

The approach

Before, after, control, impact

In medicine, you record a patient's vital signs before surgery so you can tell afterward what the operation changed. Environmental science uses the same logic, called a before-after-control-impact design. Measure a site before disturbance and a matching site that will stay untouched, then measure both again later. Only the difference between them shows the true impact.

This mission collects the "before" column now, while most of the seafloor is still undisturbed. Once mining starts, the chance to measure it is gone.

Mission plan

Two arms: one at sea, one from orbit

The sea-going arm builds the mining baseline in the Clarion-Clipperton Zone. The remote arm maps where bottom trawling happens over seamounts worldwide, using satellite vessel tracking and AI, so the ship's time goes where data is thinnest.

Before6–9 months out
  • Collect existing data: published baselines, contractor survey data made public through the Seabed Authority, and the 2023 revisit of the 1979 test track.
  • Research in the international seabed area is open to all states under the Law of the Sea [8], so no coastal-state permit is needed. The plan still coordinates with the Seabed Authority and license holders so sites aren't disturbed and results can be compared.
  • Choose paired sites: areas proposed for mining matched with untouched control areas of similar depth and nodule cover.
  • Remote arm: train an AI model on satellite vessel tracks to flag bottom-trawling behavior and map its footprint over seamounts, including those with cobalt crusts.
  • Plan a long transit (the zone is far from port), crew rotation, and data storage for the large volume of imagery.
During~24 days at sea
  • Map paired sites with multibeam sonar and fly autonomous camera surveys to photograph the seafloor in high resolution.
  • Use AI annotation to count and identify animals, especially the large attached ones that recover slowest.
  • Take sediment cores for carbon, microbes, and eDNA, and water samples to detect species not seen on camera.
  • Deploy moored current and turbidity sensors to model how far a sediment plume from mining would drift.
  • Revisit the 1979 test track to extend the longest recovery record in the deep sea.
AfterFirst 90 days
  • Publish the full baseline openly: images, species counts, sediment data, and plume model.
  • Estimate recovery timelines for each animal group, with honest uncertainty, and a plume footprint for different mining scenarios.
  • Release the global trawl-footprint map, highlighting seamounts where trawling and mining interest overlap.
  • Share results with every party in the decision: the Seabed Authority, national regulators through public comment, industry, and conservation groups.
  • Turn it into a public explainer and livestream material, so people can see the deep seafloor for themselves.

Measuring success

How we'll know it worked

QuestionMeasure
Is the baseline usable?Control and impact sites are matched closely enough that a future resurvey could detect real change.
Do we know more about recovery?Recovery estimates exist for each major animal group, with uncertainty ranges.

Why it matters

Informed consent for the planet

No surgeon operates without baseline vitals and informed consent. The deep seafloor is the largest habitat on Earth, and much of it has never been seen. Whatever the world decides about mining and trawling, it should decide knowing what is there, how it recovers, and what will be lost. Gathering that evidence, and showing it plainly to everyone, is a job an independent exploration organization is built to do.
