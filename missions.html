Mission concept 01 · Invasive species

Find the lionfish front before it moves.

Invasive lionfish are spreading along Brazil's coast faster than managers can remove them. This mission predicts where they will establish next, then sends a ship to test that prediction, so limited removal effort goes where it will do the most good.

Region: southwestern Atlantic, Brazil Planned length: ~10 days at sea Author: Sungwon Park
This is a planning exercise, not an expedition that happened. It shows how I would plan the data collection for a mission like this. The approach is modeled on OceanX's 2025 Cabo Verde: Digital Deep expedition, which mapped seafloor complexity to show where coral communities can thrive and used AI to identify species and habitats in real time. Here, the same tools ask the opposite question: where will an invader thrive?
The diagnosis

An invasion with a moving edge

Lionfish are Indo-Pacific predators with no natural control in the Atlantic. After overrunning the Caribbean, they reached Brazil. Every new reef they settle loses small native fish, and once a population is established, removal becomes a permanent job.

The constraint is the same one a hospital faces in an outbreak: there isn't enough staff to check everywhere. Removal effort is finite, so the question that matters most is where the invader will show up next. Answer that early and managers can act while a population is still small.

The approach

Borrow the coral playbook, and flip it

At Cabo Verde, OceanX used high-resolution seafloor maps to measure how complex the bottom is, a strong clue to where coral communities can thrive. Lionfish also choose habitat by its structure. A study of Caribbean reefs found they gather around large crevices and overhangs, at scales of roughly 30 to 120 cm, while avoiding fine-grained clutter [2].

Measure at the right resolution In the same study, a single overall roughness score did not separate lionfish sites from the rest of the reef [2]. Their preference only appeared when complexity was measured at several scales. Like a blood test, the reading is only useful at the right resolution, so the model here measures structure at several scales, not one.

The habitat model combines that multi-scale seafloor structure with depth, bottom temperature and salinity (which the 2025 study identified as key predictors of spread [1]), and distance from known sightings. Its output is a ranked map: every unsurveyed patch of reef scored from most to least likely to hold lionfish.

The loop runs daily at sea: each night's results re-rank the next day's sites, the same way Cabo Verde's AI models were retrained during the expedition.

What is eDNA? Every animal sheds traces of DNA into the water. Filtering a few liters of seawater and testing it for lionfish DNA shows whether one was recently nearby, even when no camera saw it. It works like a blood test for the ocean: it detects the problem before it's visible.
Mission plan

Before, during, and after

Most of the work that decides whether an expedition succeeds happens before the ship leaves port.

Before6 months to departure
  • Submit research requests to Brazil early. Under the UN Law of the Sea, foreign research in a coastal state's waters needs its consent, requested at least six months ahead [4].
  • Partner with Brazilian universities and the managers of the invaded protected areas, so the results feed straight into their decisions.
  • Build the first habitat model from existing seafloor maps, temperature and salinity records, and confirmed sightings.
  • Pick survey sites in three tiers: high, medium, and low predicted suitability. Low-tier sites are the control; a model that's never tested where it says "no" can't be trusted.
  • Plan the weather window, port calls, and sample-export permits, and set up a shared dashboard for site status and results.
During~10 days at sea
  • Map candidate areas with multibeam sonar to capture seafloor structure at several scales.
  • Fly ROV transects at each site, including deep reefs (roughly 30–150 m) that divers can't reach for culling [3].
  • Run AI image annotation on ROV video to flag lionfish and their habitat in real time.
  • Collect eDNA samples at every site, with clean blank samples to catch contamination.
  • Each night, score the day's results against the predictions and re-rank the next day's sites.
AfterFirst 30 days back
  • Score the model: how often were high-tier sites positive compared with low-tier sites?
  • Deliver a ranked priority map to protected-area managers showing where removal effort pays off most.
  • Publish the data openly and hold a post-expedition operations review to improve the next mission.
  • Turn the findings into a public explainer on this site, and run sessions for local students and fishing communities.

Measuring success

How we'll know it worked

QuestionMeasure
Does the model predict?Lionfish detection rate at high-tier sites is clearly higher than at low-tier sites.
Do the tests agree?ROV sightings and eDNA results match at most sites; mismatches get reviewed, not ignored.
Was it fast enough to matter?Results re-ranked the next day's plan at least once during the mission.
Did anyone use it?Managers change where they focus removal based on the priority map.

Why it matters

Screening, not just treatment

Medicine learned long ago that finding a disease early beats treating it late. Invasive species work the same way. A habitat model is the screening test, the ship is the follow-up exam, and the priority map is the treatment plan. Run as a loop, each mission makes the next one sharper, and the data, shared openly and explained plainly, helps the communities who live with the invasion act on it.
