A plain-language health check from published measurements

The ocean is running a fever. Here is its chart.

You don't need a degree in oceanography to read a patient's vital signs. This page walks through six of the ocean's: temperature, acidity, oxygen, coral, sea level, and fish. Each one compares where it was with where it is now. Every one of them is moving the wrong way.

Temperature

Vital sign 1 of 6

How warm the ocean's surface water is, averaged across the whole planet.

The sea surface is about 1 °C warmer than before the industrial era, and recent years are the hottest ever measured.

Global average sea surface warming since about 1900

In everyday terms One degree sounds small, but it's the difference between you at 37 °C and you at 38 °C: a low-grade fever, all the time, for an entire planet's worth of water.
Why does one degree matter so much?

Water holds enormous amounts of heat, so warming the whole ocean by even a fraction of a degree takes a staggering amount of energy. Most marine animals are cold-blooded and tuned to narrow temperature ranges. Warming pushes species toward the poles, fuels stronger storms, and triggers marine heatwaves that can kill coral and shellfish in weeks.

Where does this number come from?

Millions of readings from ships, buoys, and satellites, combined by agencies like NASA, NOAA, and Copernicus. 2023 and 2024 set records for the warmest sea surface temperatures ever directly measured.

Acidity

Vital sign 2 of 6

Seawater chemistry, measured as pH. Lower pH means more acidic water.

The ocean is roughly 30% more acidic than it was two centuries ago, the fastest chemical shift in millions of years.

Surface ocean pH: small numbers, big change

pH is a compressed scale, like earthquake magnitude, so a drop of about 0.15 units means about 30% more acidity. Global mean surface pH fell from 8.11 in 1985 to 8.04 in 2024.

In everyday terms Imagine a steady drip of soda water into every glass of water on Earth, forever. Creatures that build shells, like oysters, crabs, corals, and tiny plankton, now build them in water that slowly dissolves their building material.
What is pH, in one paragraph?

pH measures how acidic or basic a liquid is, from 0 (very acidic, like battery acid) to 14 (very basic, like drain cleaner), with 7 as neutral. Seawater sits slightly basic, around 8. Because the scale is logarithmic, each small step is a big change in chemistry.

Why is it happening?

The ocean absorbs roughly a quarter to a third of the carbon dioxide we emit. That CO₂ reacts with seawater to form carbonic acid, the same fizz that makes soda tangy. It's a favor the ocean does for our climate, but it costs the ocean its own chemistry and removes the carbonate that shell-building animals depend on.

Oxygen

Vital sign 3 of 6

Dissolved oxygen: what every fish, crab, and whale-food organism breathes.

The ocean has lost about 2% of its oxygen since 1960, and low-oxygen "dead zones" have multiplied many times over.

Two ways to see oxygen loss

2% sounds small until you remember it's 2% of all the breathable oxygen in the entire ocean, and the loss is concentrated where life is densest.

In everyday terms It's like slowly lowering the oxygen in a crowded building. Most people cope at first, but the weakest struggle, everyone is more stressed, and some rooms become impossible to stay in.
Why is the ocean losing oxygen?

Warm water holds less oxygen, like a warm soda going flat faster. Warm surface water also floats on top of cold deep water, so less fresh oxygen mixes down. Near coasts, fertilizer runoff feeds algae blooms that die, sink, and rot, using up the oxygen that remains.

What is a dead zone?

An area where oxygen is too low for most animals to survive. Fish flee, crowding into smaller habitats, and animals that can't move, like clams and worms, die. The Gulf of Mexico hosts one of the largest, roughly the size of a small U.S. state most summers.

Coral reefs

Vital sign 4 of 6

The "rainforests of the sea," home to about a quarter of all marine species on under 1% of the seafloor.

Roughly half the world's coral has died since the 1950s, and the latest global bleaching event exposed 84% of reef area to bleaching-level heat.

Living coral cover, then and now

In everyday terms Imagine half the world's cities had crumbled since your grandparents' childhood, and the rest now caught fire every few summers. That's a reef's view of the last 70 years.
What is coral bleaching?

Coral is an animal that hosts microscopic algae inside its body. The algae provide most of its food and all of its color. When water stays too hot for too long, the coral expels its algae, turns bone-white, and begins to starve. If the heat passes quickly it can recover. If heatwaves come back to back, as they now do, it dies.

Why should someone far from the tropics care?

Reefs shelter young fish we eat, buffer coastlines against storm waves, support tourism economies, and are a source of new medicines. Losing them is like losing infrastructure, pantry, and pharmacy at once for roughly a billion people.

Sea level

Vital sign 5 of 6

How high the ocean sits against our coastlines, driven by melting ice and by water expanding as it warms.

Seas have risen about 23 cm since 1900, and the rise is now more than twice as fast as in the 1990s.

Not just rising, but accelerating

In everyday terms A few millimeters a year is a bathtub filling drop by drop while someone keeps opening the tap wider. You don't notice day to day. Then a storm arrives on top of the higher water, and streets that never flooded are underwater.
Why does warm water raise sea level?

Two causes. Melting land ice from glaciers and the Greenland and Antarctic ice sheets adds new water to the ocean. And water expands as it warms, so a warming ocean takes up more room even without any new water.

Who feels it first?

Low-lying island nations, delta cities, and anyone on a coast during a high tide plus a storm. "Sunny-day flooding," where streets flood at high tide with no storm at all, is now routine in cities that rarely saw it decades ago.

Fish populations

Vital sign 6 of 6

The share of the world's assessed fish stocks being caught faster than they can reproduce.

Overfished stocks have climbed from 10% in 1974 to nearly 4 in 10 by 2023.

Share of fish stocks fished beyond sustainable limits

In everyday terms Think of a savings account. Fish populations are the principal; the catch is the interest. Sustainable fishing lives off the interest. Overfishing spends the principal, and as the account shrinks, so does the interest.
What does "overfished" actually mean?

A stock is overfished when it's caught faster than it can replace itself, so the population declines year after year. It doesn't mean the fish are gone. The good news: well-managed stocks recover, which makes this the most fixable vital sign on this page.

Why it's more than a seafood problem

Billions of people rely on the ocean as a major protein source, and hundreds of millions of livelihoods depend on fishing. Removing big predatory fish also rewires food webs, with effects that ripple down to plankton and up to seabirds and whales.

Six vital signs. One direction.

Warmer. More acidic. Less oxygen. Half the coral. Higher, faster-rising seas. Nearly four times the overfishing. Each measurement comes from a different branch of science and different instruments, and they all tell the same story, because they share the same causes: the carbon dioxide we emit and the pressure we put on marine life.

That shared cause is also the hopeful part. These aren't six separate diseases needing six cures. Cutting emissions slows the fever, the acid, the oxygen loss, the bleaching, and the rising seas together, and fisheries have shown they can rebound when given the chance. The patient is declining, but the diagnosis is clear and the treatment is known.
