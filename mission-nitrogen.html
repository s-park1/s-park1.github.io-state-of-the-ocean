Mission concept 02 · Nutrient pollution

From farm field to scallop bed.

Nitrogen washes off the land, feeds algae blooms in the estuary, clouds the water, strips oxygen, and kills the seagrass that young fish and scallops need. This mission follows that chain from start to finish in North Carolina, and forecasts what each bloom will cost the coast before it arrives.

Region: Neuse River and Pamlico Sound, NC Planned length: one bloom season, April–October Author: Sungwon Park
This is a planning exercise, not a project that happened. It shows how I would plan a land-and-coast mission. Unlike Mission 01, most of the work happens from shore: river sensors, small boats, drones, and satellites, with the people who farm, fish, and live along the water as partners.
The patient

The North Carolina bay scallop

In 1928, North Carolina led the nation in bay scallops, landing 1.4 million pounds of meat. By 2004 the harvest had fallen below 150 pounds [1]. The fishery has not come back: in 2025 no area met the minimum abundance needed to open a season, and in 2026 there was no open season at all [2].

Bay scallops live about 20 months and depend on high-salinity seagrass beds [1]. A single bad year can wipe out a local population. That makes them an early-warning species, the way a sensitive lab value flags trouble before the rest of the body shows it.

Differential diagnosis

More than one cause, so measure each one

A good diagnosis doesn't blame the first suspect. The scallop decline has several documented causes, and nitrogen is connected to some of them but not all. The mission's job is to estimate how much of the decline nitrogen explains, so money and effort go to the cause that matters most.

The approach

Follow the nitrogen, measure every link

The mission treats the watershed and estuary as one system and puts a measurement at every step, so a change at the start of the chain can be traced to its effect at the end.

Two linked models sit on top of this chain: one forecasts blooms and low oxygen from river nitrogen and weather, the other turns that forecast into expected damage to habitat and the coastal economy.

Forecasts like this already work. NOAA predicts the size of the Gulf dead zone each summer from the nitrogen the Mississippi delivers. In 2025 it measured 4,402 square miles, against a goal of under 1,900 square miles by 2035 [6], and more than 70% of that nitrogen comes from agriculture [7]. North Carolina researchers already forecast summer low oxygen in the Neuse estuary from river flow [8]. This mission connects those forecasts to the species and businesses that feel the damage.

The economics

Putting a price on a bloom

Two studies show why the impact model matters, and why it has to be more than a simple formula.

Low oxygen changes seafood prices A Duke and NOAA study of 1990–2010 found the Gulf dead zone pushes up the price of large brown shrimp relative to small ones, because hypoxia changes what fishers catch [9]. The damage shows up in markets, not just in the water.
Fear can cost more than the bloom Florida's 2018 red tide cost an estimated $2.7 billion in tourism revenue. Losses weren't proportional to algae levels: some of the largest daily losses came at low concentrations, which the authors link to public perception [10]. Clear, accurate public information is itself an economic tool.
Mission plan

Before, during, and after

BeforeWinter, 4–6 months out
  • Partner with NC environmental and fisheries agencies, university estuary labs, county extension offices, farm groups, and fishing communities.
  • Gather existing records: river flow and water quality, rainfall, land use, seagrass surveys, scallop surveys, landings, and tourism data.
  • Agree on access with landowners and on how farm data is shared. Trust comes first; the goal is targeted help, not blame.
  • Build the first bloom forecast and impact model from past seasons, and set up a shared dashboard for agencies and partners.
DuringApril–October
  • Run continuous nitrate, oxygen, and water clarity sensors at river mouths and over scallop and seagrass habitat.
  • Sample intensively after heavy rain, when most nitrogen reaches the estuary in a few days.
  • Track blooms with satellite imagery and boat sampling; map seagrass and scallop beds with drones, using AI to classify imagery.
  • Invite fishers and residents to report blooms and fish kills, adding eyes on the water between sensors.
  • Issue a forecast every week: expected bloom risk, low-oxygen risk, and estimated economic exposure for the next two weeks.
AfterFirst 60 days
  • Score each forecast against what happened, and retrain both models.
  • Estimate how much of the scallop and seagrass decline nitrogen explains, with honest uncertainty ranges.
  • Map the fields and streams where reducing nitrogen would protect the most habitat, so practices and funding can be targeted precisely.
  • Publish open data, hold a post-season review, and share results in plain language with farming and fishing communities alike.

Measuring success

How we'll know it worked

QuestionMeasure
Did the forecasts hold up?Predicted bloom and low-oxygen risk matched what sensors and satellites observed, inside the stated uncertainty.
Is the diagnosis clearer?A defensible estimate, with ranges, of how much nitrogen explains seagrass and scallop loss compared with other causes.
Did people use it?Agencies, extension offices, or farmers use the targeting map to decide where to act.
Did the public understand it?Forecast bulletins reached coastal businesses and residents in plain language before blooms peaked.

Why it matters

Treat the cause, not just the symptom

Restocking scallops without fixing the water is treating a symptom. This mission connects the symptom on the coast to its cause on land, puts a number on what it costs, and shows exactly where action will help most. It also brings together communities that rarely sit at the same table: the people who farm the watershed and the people who fish the sound. Both depend on the same water.
