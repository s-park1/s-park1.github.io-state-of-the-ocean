Mission concept 04 · Coral restoration

Plant coral where it can live.

Restoration fails when coral is planted into the same conditions that killed the reef. This mission screens for the places most likely to survive the coming decades, proves it in the water, and then watches those sites closely enough to stop the causes of coral death before they spread.

Region: screened globally, piloted in the Western Indian Ocean Planned length: screening year + ~14 days at sea Author: Sungwon Park
This is a planning exercise that builds on OceanX's own coral work. In 2025, OceanX mapped seafloor complexity at Cabo Verde's Nola Seamounts to show where coral communities can thrive, and documented coral gardens during its Around Africa expedition [1]. This concept takes the next step: from finding where coral can live to deciding where to restore it.
The hard lesson

Planting more coral isn't enough

In 2023, a record marine heatwave hit the Florida Keys, home to one of the world's largest restoration programs. At the restoration sites surveyed afterward, fewer than 22% of about 1,500 outplanted staghorn corals and fewer than 5% of about 1,000 elkhorn corals survived [2]. The corals were healthy when planted. The site and the timing failed them.

Florida's managers drew three lessons: favor slower-growing, sturdier species; protect genetic diversity, especially heat-tolerant corals; and move corals before heat arrives, because moving them during a heatwave adds stress [2]. This mission builds all three in from the start.

The diagnosis

What kills coral, and how to catch it early

Bleaching happens when heat-stressed coral expels the algae that feed it. It can recover if the heat passes, but repeated or prolonged stress kills it. Heat is the main threat, but most reefs die from heat plus something else. Local stressors are the part a restoration team can actually control.

The approach

Match the donor to the recipient

A transplant succeeds when the organ matches the patient and the patient's other conditions are under control. Coral restoration works the same way. The mission screens sites the way a transplant team screens recipients, in four narrowing steps.

Each step is cheaper than the one below it, so expensive fieldwork only goes to sites that have already passed the desk screening.

A 2026 machine-learning study mapped more than 165,000 km² of climate-resilient reefs across 71 countries and sorted them into three kinds of refuge: places that avoid heat because of cool water, places with heat-tolerant coral that resists it, and places that recover quickly afterward [6]. Step 1 starts from that map. Step 3 tests whether a site's coral really tolerates heat using a portable, low-cost system that heats coral samples on the boat and measures their thermal limit in about a day [7].

Your question

Is the African coast the best place to start?

Africa has two of the strongest candidates anywhere, but "the African coast" covers very different reefs. Here's how the leading options compare.

Recommendation

Don't commit to a coast first; let the screening decide. For a pilot, the northern Mozambique Channel is the strongest African candidate: it combines natural heat protection with high diversity, and it builds on the African research network OceanX formed during its 2025 Around Africa expedition, which involved 69 scientists from 31 countries [1].

Treat the northern Red Sea differently: as a source of heat-tolerant genetics to study and protect, not a place to expect unlimited resilience. Then compare the African shortlist honestly against the global leaders before scaling up.

Mission plan

Before, during, and after

BeforeScreening year
  • Score candidate reefs on heat history, local stressors, connectivity to healthy reefs, and whether a local partner can run the site for a decade or more.
  • Partner with national research institutes, fishing communities, and protected-area managers; restoration without local stewards doesn't last.
  • Apply for research and coral-handling permits in each country, and plan any coral transport well ahead of the hot season.
  • Shortlist about six reefs for field verification.
During~14 days at sea, outside the hot season
  • Map each shortlisted reef in high resolution to measure structure and exposure.
  • Run on-board heat-tolerance tests on coral from each site to find the hardiest colonies.
  • Survey fish, coral, disease, and seaweed with AI-annotated imagery, and sample eDNA for species cameras miss.
  • Install temperature, light, and turbidity loggers that stay behind for year-round monitoring.
  • Train local teams on the monitoring routine before the ship leaves.
AfterOngoing, with a 1-year review
  • Select the final two or three restoration sites and the heat-tolerant colonies to propagate.
  • Run a response playbook tied to heat alerts: watch, prepare, protect, and rescue (below).
  • Publish open data and a public explainer; bring students and fishers into monitoring.
  • Review after one year: survival by site, species, and genotype, and adjust before scaling.
Prevention

A response playbook, set before the heat arrives

Florida's hardest lesson was timing. Every action here is triggered by NOAA heat-stress levels [4], so the team acts on a forecast, not on bleached coral.

Heat alertWhat it meansAction
WatchWater warming above normalIncrease monitoring; check loggers and disease surveys weekly.
WarningBleaching possiblePause new planting; move or shade nursery corals now, before stress builds.
Level 1 (4+ °C-weeks)Reef-wide bleaching likelyBleaching surveys; ask partners to ease local fishing and runoff pressure.
Level 2+ (8+ °C-weeks)Mortality of sensitive corals expectedRescue the most valuable genotypes to protected nurseries; record which colonies survive, since survivors are the next generation's stock.

Risks

What could go wrong, and the plan for each

RiskMitigation
Even refuges get overwhelmedSpread risk across several sites and refuge types; don't bet on one reef.
Heat-tolerant coral trades off other traitsKeep genetic diversity; track growth and disease, not only heat survival.
Local stewardship fadesFund local roles, not just the expedition; choose partners with long-term mandates.
Moving coral spreads diseaseQuarantine and health checks before any transfer between sites.
Why it matters

Restore where the patient can recover

Restoration resources are scarce, and every coral planted in the wrong place is a loss twice over. Choosing sites with the same care a transplant team chooses a recipient, then treating the conditions that would undo the work, is how restoration stops being a gesture and starts being a recovery.
